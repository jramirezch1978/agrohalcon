Project Details

How AllpaShift works, the tools we used and the goals behind it.

How does it work?

The farmer picks a plot and sets priorities → the app (web, Android or iOS) calls the AllpaShift API → the API answers from the database if the result is fresh, or asks the calculation service, which reads NASA data and applies fixed rules → the app shows what to do and why.
  1. Pick the plot. The farmer taps a point on the map or uses the phone's GPS (native permission on Android and iOS). It works at any coordinate on Earth.
  2. Set the priority.NEW The farmer chooses what matters most: take less risk, save water or care for the soil. This changes the weights of the final score and the recommended rotation, and the app says why.
  3. Get the data. Rainfall, temperature and climatology from NASA POWER for any point. In the Piura pilot area it adds GPM IMERG satellite rainfall, SMAP soil moisture and MODIS NDVI vegetation; on the northern coast, MUR SST sea temperature.
  4. Know the local soil.NEW The farmer marks the soil type (sandy, loam, clay) and salinity of the plot. The app suggests the soil type from SoilGrids (ISRIC, sand/silt/clay at 0–30 cm), plus organic carbon (organic matter) and infiltration, and labels it as such, never as NASA data. SMAP adds the current soil moisture.
  5. Test a dry or wet year.NEW A what-if scenario (dry: −40 % rain, wet: +60 % rain over the NASA POWER normal) shows how the season risk and the rotations would change. It is clearly marked as a scenario, not a forecast.
  6. Compute the indicators. Rainfall, soil moisture, heat, El Niño and vegetation compare the current value with the normal for that month and get a level: low, medium or high.
  7. Estimate season risk. For each crop, from planting to harvest, the climate of each month is compared with what the crop tolerates. If sea temperature signals El Niño, rain risk from January to April goes up.
  8. Score the rotations: climate, soil health and water.NEW The climate score starts at 100 and loses points for water needs versus deficit, months outside the temperature range, high-risk months and planting out of season. The soil-health score rewards nitrogen-fixing legumes (e.g. cowpea, lablab), alternating root depths and soil cover, and penalizes monoculture, two crops of the same family, two water-hungry crops in a row, and salt-sensitive crops on saline or sandy soil (crop traits from FAO-56 and FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper 29). The final score weights climate, soil and water by the farmer's priority. For each rotation the app lists benefits, risks and trade-offs, what we don't know, and what to check with a local expert.
  9. Decide and explain. Priority rules choose the action for this fortnight. The app shows the result with its NASA dataset, and an AI assistant explains it in plain words. The AI does no calculations.

Validation with the 2017 El Niño

We applied the same rules to real data from 2001 to 2026. Rainfall comes from GPM IMERG and the alert from the MUR SST series. Tapping any month shows its rain day by day and week by week (IMERG daily), so the farmer can see exactly when the heaviest rains fell.

What the system does not do

AllpaShift does not forecast exact weather months ahead: it estimates risk from NASA climate history and the early El Niño signal. It does not replace a soil laboratory test. Thresholds and soil-health weights are team assumptions, documented in the code so they can be adjusted.

What tools did we use?

ComponentTools
App (web, Android, iOS)Vue 3, Vite, TypeScript, Tailwind CSS, Pinia, vue-i18n (Spanish and English), Leaflet for the map, Chart.js for charts and Capacitor for Android and iOS.
AllpaShift APIJava 17 with Spring Boot and Spring Cloud Gateway. It is the only component that reads and writes the database, and answers from it when data is fresh.
DatabasePostgreSQL: crop catalog, zones, rotations, saved results and every NASA value used.
Calculation servicePython with FastAPI, Pydantic, httpx, pandas and NumPy. Rules and thresholds in configuration files, with automated tests (pytest). It only reads NASA data and calculates.
NASA data accessNASA POWER API; NASA Earthdata (CMR and OPeNDAP) for GPM IMERG and SMAP; ORNL DAAC MODIS web service for NDVI; MUR SST v4.1 (NASA JPL, PO.DAAC) through NOAA CoastWatch ERDDAP.
AI assistantGroq as the platform, with the open model gpt-oss-20b (Apache 2.0). It only writes explanations from results that are already calculated.
DeploymentDocker and Nginx on a cloud server.
Soil dataSoilGrids 2.0 REST API (ISRIC, CC-BY 4.0) to suggest the soil type; the farmer can change it.
Maps and collaborationOpenStreetMap data and place search (ODbL, with attribution). GitHub for code and version control.

All project code is released under the Apache 2.0 license.

Goals

  • Help farmers decide what to plant based on data, not just habit.
  • Choose rotations that protect and rebuild the soil, not only the next harvest.
  • Respect what each farmer values most: lower risk, saving water or caring for the soil.
  • Reduce harvest losses from drought, extreme rain, heat and El Niño, and warn early using sea temperature.
  • Make NASA data easy to understand for people without technical training, in any farming region of the world.
